§What this series is

For seventy years, synthesising sound meant hand-designing a signal-processing algorithm — an oscillator bank, a filter, a physical model of a string. You wrote the equations; the computer executed them. Neural sound synthesis inverts that: you supply examples of the sound you want, and a network learns the generating process itself, discovering structure no engineer wrote down.

This series is a comprehensive, technical tour of that revolution — the models, the mathematics, and the ideas that connect them. It is built for people who want to understand, not just use: every part derives its core equations, shows the architecture as an interactive diagram, and lets you hear the technique running live. It sits alongside the classical-DSP synthesis work in the DSP & Music collection — where neural methods stand on the shoulders of the vocoder, the spectrogram, and differentiable filters.

The central question of the field: an audio second is 16,000–48,000 numbers, each depending on all the others. How do you learn a probability distribution over that — and sample from it fast enough to be useful? — the thread running through all twelve parts

§The map of the field

Every generative-audio system is a choice along two axes: what domain it models the sound in (raw samples, a spectrogram, discrete tokens, or a learned latent), and what generative family it uses to place a distribution over that domain. Hover the map to trace where each landmark model sits — the whole series is a walk across this plane.

Nothing on this map is purely one thing. VITS is a flow and a VAE and a GAN; MusicGen is a language model over a codec's tokens. The families are lenses, not boxes — and the most capable modern systems deliberately combine them.

§How we judge a synthesiser

A generative-audio model cannot be scored by a single loss. The field uses a ladder of metrics — from human listening tests to embedding-space distances — and each part reports the ones that matter for its task. The two you will meet everywhere:

Perceptual: the Mean Opinion Score

Human listeners rate naturalness from 1 (bad) to 5 (excellent); the MOS is the average. Its rigorous cousin MUSHRA hides a reference and anchors among the candidates. Slow and expensive, but the only ground truth for "does this sound real?"

Objective: the Fréchet Audio Distance

FAD mirrors the FID of image generation: embed real and generated audio with a pretrained network, fit a Gaussian to each set, and measure the distance between them —

$$\mathrm{FAD}=\lVert\mu_r-\mu_g\rVert^2+\operatorname{tr}\!\left(\Sigma_r+\Sigma_g-2\left(\Sigma_r\Sigma_g\right)^{1/2}\right)$$

where $(\mu_r,\Sigma_r)$ and $(\mu_g,\Sigma_g)$ are the mean and covariance of the real and generated embeddings. Lower is better; unlike a per-sample loss it rewards matching the distribution of a corpus. Part 12 dissects it, and CLAP score — how well generated audio matches a text prompt — in full.

§How to read this series

Every part is a single self-contained web page — no installation, no accounts, no audio files downloaded. Open it and the sound is generated on your machine.

Turn the volume upThe demos are the argument. Reading about a vocoder is nothing like hearing Griffin–Lim and HiFi-GAN invert the same spectrogram.
Open the derivationsCollapsed ▸ derivation panels hold the full maths. Skip them for the intuition; open them for the proof.
Follow the cross-linksEach part ends with links to its neighbours. Diffusion (P8) makes more sense after codecs (P9) which makes more sense after vocoders (P3).
These are teaching modelsThe in-browser networks are small, real implementations of the ideas — enough to hear the mechanism — not the billion-parameter production systems. The maths is the real thing.
